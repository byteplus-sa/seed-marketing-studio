# Folklore and mythology recipe

**Depict the tradition the user supplies; no culture is a default.** The same
print treatment works for any folklore, mythology, legend, or original concept.
The cultural content comes from the user's description or verified sources,
never from the style reference or from an assumed market.

## Research before describing

- Start from the user's own variant. Many traditions hold several regional or
  family versions of a creature; the user's version wins over a generic one.
- When a precise folklore claim matters, check it against sources from or
  about the tradition itself, such as museum collections, folklore archives,
  or published scholarship. Record unresolved disagreements as open inputs
  rather than choosing silently.
- Separate what the tradition says from your artistic interpretation. Label
  interpretive choices (an object, costume, or color) as this poster's choice,
  not universal canon.
- Ask when the subject is sacred, ceremonial, or belongs to a living community
  that restricts its depiction. Do not invent sacred meanings, rituals, or
  symbols, and do not fill gaps with motifs borrowed from another culture.

## Turn lore into visible direction

For each subject, write a short row before drafting the prompt:

| Field | What to capture |
| --- | --- |
| Distinguishing anatomy | Head, body plan, limb count, connected or separated parts, scale |
| Defining object or trait | The one detail that makes the subject recognizable |
| Habitat or setting | Landscape, architecture, flora, and weather the tradition links to it |
| Composition motif | The environmental shape that carries the silhouette, such as roots, waves, wind, or coils |
| Likely failure | The generic creature the model may fall back to |

**Name the likely failure explicitly.** A horse-headed or bull-headed human
can collapse into a four-legged animal; a serpent with a mane can become a
generic dragon; a tiny spirit can be scaled up into a monster. State the body
plan positively and add one short exclusion for that specific failure.

## Choose setting cues with restraint

Pick a few setting details that the tradition actually associates with the
subject: regional architecture, plants, boats, terrain, or celestial events.
Choose only what fits the particular scene rather than inserting every cue
into every image. Keep miniature architecture in the lower portion so it
establishes scale without competing with the subject.

Do not import a style reference's creature, city, lettering, or national
setting. A reference that shows one culture's architecture supplies only its
print treatment for a poster about another tradition.

## Depict respectfully

- Prefer non-graphic, stylized ink shapes for violent or bodily folklore
  unless the brief asks otherwise.
- Avoid caricatured ethnic features, costume stereotypes, and exoticizing
  framing for human figures in the tradition.
- Keep real sacred sites, religious figures, and community emblems out of a
  commercial poster unless the user supplies them and confirms the right to
  use them.

## Worked example

The bundled [example series](../examples/README.md) applies this recipe to
six creatures from one folklore tradition. Its prompts state each body plan
before ornament, keep a horse-headed figure explicitly bipedal with human
arms, keep a connected shapeshifter distinct from a creature with separated
halves, and vary the motif per subject (smoke and roots, wings and sky, coils
and waves). Treat those descriptors as one interpretation of one tradition,
not as defaults for other subjects.
